# Academic Scout — Sources

**Research question:** Do humans really use only 10% of their brains? Is it true, and where did the myth originate?

**Web tools:** Available (WebSearch + WebFetch used). Some publisher/PDF pages (Scientific American article HTML, philarchive/ResearchGate PDFs) returned 403 or unparseable binary; for those, citation metadata was recovered from search snippets and cross-referenced bibliographies (chiefly the well-referenced Wikipedia and Univ. of Washington pages). Such rows are flagged.

**Bottom line:** The "10% myth" is false. It is an *established* scientific consensus — converging from lesion/clinical neurology, functional neuroimaging (fMRI/PET), metabolic studies, microstructural anatomy, and evolutionary reasoning — that there is no large idle fraction of the brain. The myth's exact origin is not pinned to one source; it is best documented (by Beyerstein 1999) as a 20th-century distortion of "reserve potential" psychology (William James) amplified by self-help/popular media. Origin attributions are *suggestive/historical*, not experimentally established.

## Verdict on strength of the overall conclusion
- **"We use 100% of the brain (no large idle fraction)": ESTABLISHED / replicated.** Multiple independent evidence classes converge (lesion neurology, fMRI/PET, neuroenergetics, microanatomy, evolution). No peer-reviewed source supports the 10% claim.
- **"Where the myth originated": SUGGESTIVE / historical.** Best account (Beyerstein) traces it to distorted "reserve mental potential" ideas (William James) amplified by 1929 Almanac, 1936 Carnegie/Thomas foreword, and pulp sci-fi. No definitive single origin; attributions are historical reconstructions, not experimental findings.
