# Do Humans Really Use Only 10% of Their Brains?

**A research report on one of the most persistent myths in popular science.**

*Date: 2026-06-09*

---

## Bottom Line Up Front

No. The claim that humans use only 10% of their brains is **false** — it is one of the most thoroughly debunked myths in neuroscience. Modern brain imaging, metabolic studies, evolutionary reasoning, and clinical evidence from brain damage all converge on the same conclusion: over the course of a normal day, we use virtually **all** of our brain. Johns Hopkins neurologist Barry Gordon has called the idea "so wrong it is almost laughable."

The myth most likely arose from a **misinterpretation and sensationalization** of early 20th-century psychology — specifically William James's idea that people fail to live up to their full *mental potential* — which was later distorted into the very different (and untrue) claim that 90% of the *physical brain* sits idle. A precise "10 percent" figure was attached and popularized largely through the **self-help and popular-media industry** of the 1920s–1930s, and the myth has been cemented in public consciousness by films and advertising ever since.

---

## Part 1: Is It True? The Scientific Evidence

Multiple independent lines of evidence refute the myth. No single one is the whole story, but together they are decisive.

### 1. Brain imaging shows the whole brain is active
Functional imaging technologies — **fMRI** (functional magnetic resonance imaging) and **PET** (positron emission tomography) — let scientists watch the living brain at work. They show that, no matter what a person is doing, **activity is distributed across the entire brain**. There is no large region that simply sits dormant. Even during sleep, all parts of the brain show some level of activity, regulating, monitoring, sensing, and consolidating. (PET scans typically use a radioactive glucose tracer; because the brain metabolizes glucose, the scan reveals where energy is being consumed — and the answer is: everywhere.)

### 2. The brain is metabolically far too expensive to be 90% idle
This is one of the strongest arguments. The brain is about **2% of body mass but consumes roughly 20% of the body's energy** (calories/oxygen) — proportionally more than any other organ. From an evolutionary standpoint, maintaining a large, energy-hungry organ that was 90% unused would be a catastrophic liability: a large brain also raises the risk of death in childbirth. Natural selection would have strongly favored humans with smaller, cheaper, more efficient brains. The fact that we evolved and retained such a costly organ implies the tissue is being used.

### 3. Brain damage almost always causes deficits
If 90% of the brain were unused "spare" tissue, then damage to most of it should be harmless. The clinical reality is the opposite: **there is almost no area of the brain that can be damaged without some loss of ability.** Strokes, tumors, and head injuries — even those affecting small regions — routinely cause profound, specific impairments (paralysis, loss of speech, memory loss, sensory deficits). Conditions like Parkinson's disease show that damage to even tiny structures has devastating effects.

### 4. Decades of mapping found no "functionless" regions
More than a century of research — from 19th-century lesion studies through modern electrophysiology and imaging — has steadily mapped the brain's regions and their jobs. This **localization of function** research has never turned up a large area without a known role. Different tasks recruit different regions, but across the full range of behaviors, essentially all of the brain gets used.

### 5. Microstructure and "use it or lose it" pruning
The brain is biologically efficient. During development, **synaptic pruning** eliminates unused neurons and connections — "use it or lose it." If 90% of neurons were genuinely never used, autopsies of healthy brains should reveal massive, widespread degeneration. They do not. Healthy brains show no such large-scale wasting, which is inconsistent with most of the tissue being permanently unused.

### 6. Quantitative checks
The myth even fails its own framing. When the TV program *MythBusters* used imaging to measure brain activity during a demanding mental task (2010), they reported usage far above 10% (around 35% active *at one instant* during one task — and far more over time). Mayo Clinic neurologist John Henley has summarized the consensus: **over a 24-hour period, we use 100% of the brain.**

> **An important nuance — what's actually true:** While we use all of our brain *anatomically*, it is fair to say most people have untapped *potential* in the sense of skills, knowledge, and discipline they could still develop. That is a statement about learning and effort, not about dormant brain tissue. Conflating the two is exactly the error at the heart of the myth.

> **A genuine open frontier (not the myth):** Roughly 90% of brain cells are **glial cells** rather than neurons, and their functions are still being uncovered. This is sometimes confused with the myth, but it is unrelated — glial cells are active and essential; they are simply less understood, not "unused."

## Part 2: Where Did the Myth Come From?

There is no single documented "patient zero" for this myth. Eric Chudler, a University of Washington neuroscientist who investigated it, candidly concluded: *"I have not been able to track down the exact source of this myth."* What historians **can** reconstruct is a chain of plausible roots and the people who popularized it.

### Root: A misread of William James (c. 1907–1908)
The most commonly cited origin is the Harvard psychologist **William James**. Around 1907–1908 (in works including his essay/address *The Energies of Men*), James wrote that "**we are making use of only a small part of our possible mental and physical resources.**" James — along with colleague **Boris Sidis** and their "reserve energy" theories — was making a point about **unrealized human potential**, not about idle brain matter.

The critical distortion: James's statement about untapped *potential* was later reinterpreted as a claim that 90% of the *physical brain* is unused. These are very different claims, and the myth is the result of collapsing the first into the second.

### The "10 percent" figure gets attached and popularized (1920s–1930s)
James never specified a percentage. The precise number appears to have crystallized through popular and self-help media:

- **1929** — *The World Almanac* reportedly carried a line to the effect that "scientists and psychologists tell us we use only about ten percent of our brain power."
- **1932** — Science-fiction writer **John W. Campbell** wrote that "no man in all history ever used even half of the thinking part of his brain," reflecting (and spreading) the idea in popular fiction.
- **1936** — The most influential vector: broadcaster and writer **Lowell Thomas**, in his foreword to **Dale Carnegie's** mega-bestselling self-help book *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, wrote that "Professor William James of Harvard used to say that the average man develops only ten percent of his latent mental ability." This **falsely attributed a specific 10% figure to James** and rode along with a book that has sold tens of millions of copies.
- **1970s** — Bulgarian psychologist **Georgi Lozanov**, promoting his "suggestopedia" learning method, similarly suggested we might use only five to ten percent of our mental capacity, keeping the idea alive in education circles.

### The Einstein attribution is apocryphal
A popular version claims **Albert Einstein** explained his genius by saying he used more than the usual 10% of his brain. There is **no evidence he ever said this.** In 2004, skeptic and psychologist **Barry Beyerstein** asked the **Albert Einstein Archives** (at Caltech) to search for the quote; they found **no record** of any such statement. The Einstein attribution appears to be a later embellishment that lent false authority to the myth.

### Other proposed (weaker) roots
Some accounts point to possible misunderstandings of older neuroscience — e.g., 19th-century work by **Pierre Flourens**, or **Karl Lashley's** 1920s–30s rat-brain ablation experiments, or a 1930s confusion about the relatively sparse "local" (active) neurons versus the more numerous support cells. These are speculative; the James-misinterpretation-plus-self-help-popularization story is the best supported.

## Part 3: Why the Myth Persists

Despite being debunked for decades, the belief remains remarkably widespread — it is a classic **"neuromyth."**

- **Prevalence among the public and even educators is high.** Surveys of teachers around the world have found large fractions endorsing the myth — for example, roughly **46–48%** of English and Dutch teacher samples in one well-known study believed we use only 10% of our brains. Studies comparing groups have found the general public endorsing the most neuromyths, with educators somewhat fewer, and those with high neuroscience exposure fewer still — but **even neuroscience training does not fully eliminate the belief.**
- **It's an appealing idea.** The myth implies a hidden reservoir of untapped power waiting to be unlocked — an attractive premise for self-improvement marketing, psychics, and "brain training" products.
- **It misreads everyday experience.** As Barry Gordon notes, people interpret their own limitations and failures as evidence of dormant, unused brain capacity, rather than recognizing that they're already using the whole organ.
- **Hollywood keeps it alive.** Films built on the premise — *Flight of the Navigator* (1986), *Defending Your Life* (1991), *Powder* (1995), *Limitless* (2011, which uses a 20% variant), and especially *Lucy* (2014) — repackage the myth for new audiences as a thrilling "what if."

---

## Conclusion

The "10% of the brain" claim is a **myth**, not a fact. We use essentially all of our brain — different parts for different tasks, and the whole of it over the course of normal activity. The evidence is overwhelming and multi-pronged: imaging shows whole-brain activity; the brain's enormous energy cost makes idleness evolutionarily implausible; damage to almost any region causes deficits; and a century of mapping has found no large unused area.

The myth most plausibly grew out of a **misinterpretation of William James's century-old point about untapped human *potential***, was given a false precision ("10 percent") and mass exposure through the **1920s–1930s self-help and popular-media industry** (notably Lowell Thomas's foreword to Dale Carnegie's 1936 bestseller), and was later dressed up with an **apocryphal Einstein quote**. Its survival owes more to its motivational appeal and entertainment value than to anything in science.
